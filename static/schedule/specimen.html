Weight is width, tone and medium together. The scale changes hands in the middle: the two heavy weights are ink and stay solid, because a firm line is solid; the two light ones are graphite and are broken up, because graphite does not get lighter, it gets sparser. That transition is the place the drawing comes alive, and it is visible above between the object line and the hairline. Nothing here is bold; things are heavier.

The pair below the scale is the argument, drawn twice. Both rows carry the same mean coverage. On the left it is made of near-opaque particles with paper between them, the way a pencil only touches the peaks of the tooth — in design-references/02 the palest ruled wall averages 222 and its darkest particle measures 0. On the right it is the flat alpha that average implies, which is what the system used to be all the way down, and is most of why it read as rendered. Turn Texture off above and the left-hand pair becomes the right-hand one.

02b Media Three, and the difference is meant to be small
Ink — warm

Object lines, cut lines, type. The subject of the drawing, and the only medium that is ever solid.

--dr-ink-rgb
Graphite — cool

Setting-out, the construction layer, hatching, stipple. Everything ruled before the drawing was inked.

--dr-graphite-rgb
Wash — warm, dilute

Tonal areas only, and never a line — a wash has no edge of its own, it stops where it stops.

--dr-wash-rgb

The top swatch of each column is the medium at full strength, where the hue is obvious. Everything below it is that medium at the strength it is actually used at, where the hue should not be: about six points warm for ink, three points cool for graphite, five points warm for a wash. That is the difference between two pencils in a box, not a colour code.

The test is one question — name the hue of a hairline without putting it beside an object line. If you can, it has gone too far and the three triplets want moving back towards each other. Which medium a mark is drawn in says what kind of mark it is, and that is the same thing its weight already says, so the two never disagree: there is no warm hairline and no cool cut anywhere in the system.

Five materials, told apart by pitch and angle so they stay distinct in a screenshot and to a colourblind reader. Never a flat fill. Stipple is the lightest, which is why the band most blocks sit on gets it.

The tone comes from the pitch, not from faint strokes: every hatch stroke here is near-solid graphite and the spacing carries the value, which is what the masonry in design-references/02 does — a mid-grey wall made of black lines. Faint strokes at a tight pitch give the same average and read as a screen tint. Stipple is the one tone that is genuinely sparse rather than widely spaced, and it is the one that is a raster.

The overlap swatch is the reason none of them is a fill: where working and domestic hours cover the same time, both tones survive and the eye reads both materials at once. Give either band an opaque background and that stops being true — which is exactly why it does not happen on the neumorphic schedule, where the working band is a solid --sunken.

What a block is is said by its edge, never by a fill and never by a hue. A task carries the heaviest line in the system on its spine, because it is the subject of the drawing. Timetabled is a double rule — a given, fixed. Prep is bracketed, not boxed; a break is hatched with no edge at all; travel is not a block but the line between two. Locked takes a datum mark, at risk takes a red spine and a flag, ghosted is outline only in construction ink. "Next up" is the loudest mark the system allows: a red bracket set outside the block, so it reads as an annotation pointing at it.

Closer to a plan than a section: a grid of cells, a plate number in each corner, and no block drawn — a month of packed rectangles says nothing. What it carries instead is the four things a month is for. Load is read as tone, the working band's stipple tightened step by step against the day's own hours, never as a counted list. A deadline is a red rule at the head of its day, its weight the importance — hairline, object or structural, the three the system already has. The finishing buffer before a hand-in is hatched and dashed: held, not merely busy. An at-risk day takes the red edge and the flag, the same as an at-risk block.

And this is where the construction geometry earns its keep: compass arcs are struck back from each deadline and projection lines run across its week and down its column, so the run-up is drawn as geometry rather than counted in cells. All of it is one overlay carrying .dr-construction — take the layer off and every tone, rule, flag and buffer is still there. Crosshatch stays "not our material": the days outside the month.

Three things make setting-out read as setting-out rather than as a second, sloppier line. It is coincident — the same line, not one beside it. It overruns, by 18px against the object rules' own 7, which is what says the grid was ruled across the sheet before anything was drawn on it. And a circle is struck whole, with its centre marked and its radius drawn, because that is what a compass leaves behind; the arc is only the part that got inked.

It is also thinner than what it constructs — half a pixel against the object line's one — and it is a pencil, not a blur: the geometry is exact and only the density varies.

Ruled on the left, drawn on the right — and the right-hand column is what Texture in the title block turns off, so every pair here is the same switch shown one primitive at a time. Nothing on this sheet may stop being readable when the left column is what the surface renders.

Three of these are now vendored 512px rasters rather than procedural noise: the paper tooth, the graphite stipple and the wash. Turbulence is band-limited Perlin, so its features are round and its histogram symmetric, and paper is neither — a sheet has directional cellulose fibre, a slow cloudiness from how the pulp settled, and a heavy tail of dark flecks. None of those is a sum of Perlin octaves, which is most of why the procedural sheet read as noise laid over a UI. The tiles are seamless by construction (an inverse FFT of a periodic spectrum is periodic), total 160 KB, and are generated rather than scanned — see static/vendor/textures/README.md.

Turbulence keeps the jobs it is better at. Every line in the system is still an SVG data URI, because a line needs exact ruled geometry at retina resolution and a raster cannot give it — so the hatches vary in density rather than in geometry, and the setting-out holds its position exactly while a mask eats into its alpha. Displacing a hairline smears it across three device pixels and reads as blur and as extra width, which is the one failure worse than flat.

The last pair is the one that decided the architecture — two hatches crossing, laid over each other normally and then multiplied. They are near enough identical, because dark ink on light paper already darkens under ordinary compositing: 0.2 over 0.2 is 0.36. So the surface does not blend every tone, which would cost a compositing layer per band and per arc for a difference nobody can see. It spends blending on the two places it changes the picture: the grain, which must darken the sheet rather than veil it, and the wash, which is colour laid over ink. Nothing that moves carries it.

A task that will quietly reappear once it is done, and one that will not, are different things and must not draw the same. The recurring block keeps every object line it had but its trailing edge goes dashed — not closed off, it comes round again — and a letterspaced tag sits in the low corner opposite the deliverable index, carrying the interval and nothing else. No hue: this language has none to spend on it, so the cadence is a word, the way the month's buffer is Reserved. The full phrasing — interval, tolerance, whether it is paused — is a row in the detail key and a line on the Tasks tab; the block only has to say that it repeats.

The month's second drawing (schedule/axonometric.js), which stands the day's work up the vertical axis so that a heavy day is a tower and an empty one is flat ground. Every point of it — these studies included — comes out of one projection function; nothing on the sheet is placed by eye, which is also what makes an orientation control later an afternoon's work rather than a rewrite. It carries no value of its own: a block is paper and object lines, a timetabled one is a step lighter with its leading edge doubled, at risk is a faint red all round and a red spine, and something drawn but claiming none of the user's time is dashed or in construction ink — section 8's vocabulary, stood on end. The deadline is the one mark that gains something in projection: a circle struck on the base plane is an ellipse, so the compass sweep the flat view fakes with a border-radius is here simply true, and it is struck whole with its centre marked and its radius drawn.
